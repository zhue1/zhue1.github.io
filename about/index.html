About · Miami → Gainesville, FL

I want to build something that matters before I leave this world.

I'm Eric, a CS senior at the University of Florida and the founder of Orbi Labs. This is the honest version of how I got here, and why I build.

Where I come from

I grew up inside my family's restaurant.

I grew up in Miami in a Chinese family that spoke Mandarin at home. We weren't wealthy. My parents ran a Japanese buffet: my mom was the manager and my dad was a sushi chef. They worked almost every day, rarely taking a break, so I could have chances they never had.

From sixth grade on I was there every day after school. I seated customers, took drink orders, cleared tables, rolled sushi, prepped the morning salads and helped with desserts. By eleventh grade it was a real job. I did my homework at a back table on my mom's old laptop, the same one I first played Roblox on. The quiet nights near closing were when I got to really talk with my mom.

Most days I went home to an empty house, so I learned early to take care of myself. We rarely traveled: China once with my mom, and New York to see family. My parents gave up their own dream of seeing the world so I could have a shot at mine.

First grade → sixth grade → ninth grade

I've wanted to be an inventor since first grade. Only the vocabulary changed.

I played Roblox with friends for years, and what hooked me was the thought that I could build something strangers would enjoy. At the start of sixth grade I opened Roblox Studio and taught myself Lua. My first build was a map stitched together from toolbox parts, and I loved it.

School never held my attention the way building did. When something interested me, I went far deeper than any class asked. In ninth grade, when COVID hit, I started an entire multiplayer RPG on my own: combat, quests, weapons, saves and enemies, 7,000+ lines of code. I once spent two weeks hunting a bug that turned out to be two variables with the same name. Finding coroutines changed how I built everything. Some fixes came to me in my sleep, and I'd wake up and write them down.

When my friends finally played it, the ragdoll physics had us laughing for hours. That's still my favorite feeling: people enjoying something I made.

The gap I couldn't ignore

I trained hard for years and still stalled.

I started with home workouts in ninth grade, using 5 and 10 lb dumbbells and resistance bands. In twelfth grade I joined a gym with friends, knowing nothing, and ran popular programs off the internet. My squat, bench and deadlift sat in the same place for almost a year, even on a bulk, and when I cut I lost strength because I didn't know how to diet. The problem wasn't effort. I didn't know how to train, eat or sleep, and a coach was always too expensive.

That same year I tried to build the fix: a Swift app called Egolyft, with sign-in, workout logging and a live session screen. I got stuck on the progress graphs and dropped it. Years later I knew far more, about lifting and about building software, so I came back to the idea. That second attempt is Ora: an AI training coach grounded in real, up-to-date research, joined with accountability, friends and game-like progress. I'm the only engineer behind it, with 700+ merged pull requests and counting.

Why I keep going

It feels less like a career and more like a calling.

Building is what keeps me going through rough stretches. I've always felt I was put here to do something that matters, and that I can't waste it. In ten years I want millions of people to stop worrying about just getting by, and to have room to enjoy the good parts of life. I hope some of them go on to do the same for others. That's what I'm building toward.

Ora is the first step: healthier, stronger people, and, with their consent, training data that can push the science of lifting forward. To move faster on my own, I built EricOS, a system that lets me work like a small team.

I want my parents to finally rest and see the world they spent their lives working for. And I want to leave this world better than I found it.
